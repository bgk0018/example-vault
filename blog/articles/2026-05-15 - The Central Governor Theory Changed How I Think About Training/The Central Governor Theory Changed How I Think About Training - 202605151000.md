# Content

I used to think fatigue was simple: muscles run out of fuel, performance drops, you stop. Then I read [[Endure - Alex Hutchinson|Endure]] and encountered the [[Central Governor Theory - 202604201415|central governor theory]], and it fundamentally changed how I approach training.

## The Old Model

The "catastrophe model" of fatigue says your body hits a wall — glycogen depleted, lactate accumulated, muscles fail. Training under this model is purely physical: build bigger fuel tanks, clear waste faster, increase muscular endurance.[^1]

## The New Model

Tim Noakes proposed something radical: the brain acts as a governor, actively limiting physical output *before* the body reaches true failure. It's a protective mechanism — the brain always holds a reserve.[^2]

This means fatigue isn't just a physical event. It's a *decision* made by the brain based on:
- Physiological signals (heart rate, core temperature, fuel status)
- Psychological factors (motivation, competition, knowledge of remaining distance)
- Prior experience (what has the brain learned to tolerate?)

## Why This Matters for Training

If the brain is the ultimate limiter, then [[Progressive Overload - 202604151030|progressive overload]] works on two fronts simultaneously:[^3]

1. **Physiological** — muscle fibers grow, capillary density increases
2. **Neurological** — the brain recalibrates its safety limits based on repeated exposure

This dual nature explains why mental rehearsal, competition, and deliberate discomfort exposure all improve performance — they're training the governor, not the muscles.

## The Practical Takeaway

For recreational athletes (like me, training for a [[Marathon Training Project|marathon]]):
- Don't just train the body — train the brain's tolerance for discomfort
- Race-day performance often exceeds training performance because competition changes the governor's calculus
- [[Sleep and Recovery - 202605011200|Sleep]] matters doubly — a tired brain is a conservative governor
